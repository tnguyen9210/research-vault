# FQI Finite-Sample Analysis (Jiang 2020 note)

> **Scope.** A line-by-line walkthrough (2026-09-01) of Nan Jiang's
> *Notes on Fitted Q-iteration* (30 Aug 2020): the
> $J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)$ bound for [[fitted-q-iteration]] under
> realizability, Bellman completeness and uniform concentrability,
> together with the Bernstein fast rate.
>
> **Assumed, not restated here:** the FQI template and the MDP
> notation, on [[fitted-q-iteration]]. **Left to other pages:** what
> the coverage assumption rules out, [[extrapolation-error]]; the
> single-policy and instance-dependent successor,
> [[fitted-q-iteration-pessimistic]]; the
> horizon-one case, where the coverage story appears without
> bootstrapping, [[contextual-bandits-offline-value-based]]. Nothing here is
> pessimistic — this is the analysis of plain FQI. §35 records what
> was checked against the source and what was not.

The proof becomes much easier once we separate it into three
questions:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{A.}&\quad
J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\|Q^*-f_k\|,\\
\text{B.}&\quad
\|Q^*-f_k\|
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|,\\
\text{C.}&\quad
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{finite-sample regression error}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

Then the proof plugs C into B and B into A. That is the entire high-level
structure of the analysis on pages 2–3.

I will follow the note line by line.

## Reading path

2489 lines in 35 sections; these are the entry points.

- **The whole argument at once** — §24 chains every step into a
  single display. Read it first for the shape, or last as a check.
- **Block A, suboptimality to $Q$-error** (§1–§7). §1–§5 reduce
  $J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)$ to an $L_2$ norm under the distributions
  that matter; §6–§7 state and prove Lemma 1, which converts value
  error into $Q$-function error.
- **Block B, the recursion** (§8–§16). The change of measure onto the
  offline distribution (§10–§12, with §12 the starred step), Jensen
  (§13), and the recursive structure that results (§16).
- **Block C, regression error** (§17–§23). Why squared loss is exactly
  Bellman error (§17), where Bellman completeness is actually used
  (§19), and the initial error $e_0$ (§22).
- **The fast rate** (§25–§31). §25 explains why the first analysis
  stalls at $n^{-1/4}$; §26–§31 redo it with Bernstein on excess loss.
- **Reference** — §33 lists where each assumption was used, §34 states
  the idea the proof turns on, and §32 records a rigor gap in the
  source together with how to close it.

## 0. Notation we need before starting

The final FQI iterate is

$$
f_k,
$$

and its greedy policy is

$$
\widehat\pi
=
\pi_{f_k},
\qquad
\widehat\pi(s)
\in
\arg\max_a f_k(s,a).
$$

The optimal Q- and value-functions are

$$
Q^*,
\qquad
V^*(s)=\max_a Q^*(s,a).
$$

The Bellman optimality operator is

$$
(Tf)(s,a)
=
\mathbb E\left[
r+\gamma V_f(s')
\mid s,a
\right],
$$

where

$$
V_f(s)=\max_a f(s,a).
$$

In particular,

$$
Q^*=TQ^*.
$$

Finally,

$$
d_h^{\widehat\pi}
$$

is the distribution of the state at time $h$ when we start from
$d_0$ and execute $\widehat\pi$.

---

## 1. Start with policy suboptimality

The proof begins with

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)
&=
\sum_{h=1}^\infty
\gamma^{h-1}
\mathbb E_{s\sim d_h^{\widehat\pi}}
\left[
V^*(s)-Q^*(s,\widehat\pi(s))
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{A}
$$

This first equality deserves quite a bit of explanation.


### Why does this equality hold?

Define

$$
\delta(s)
=
V^*(s)-Q^*(s,\widehat\pi(s)).
$$

Since

$$
Q^*(s,a)
=
\mathbb E[
r+\gamma V^*(s')
\mid s,a
],
$$

we have

$$
\delta(s)
=
V^*(s)
-
\mathbb E[
r+\gamma V^*(s')
\mid s,\widehat\pi(s)
].
$$

Now consider a trajectory generated by $\widehat\pi$:

$$
s_1,a_1,r_1,s_2,a_2,r_2,\ldots
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}
\gamma^{h-1}
\mathbb E[
V^*(s_h)-Q^*(s_h,\widehat\pi(s_h))
]
\\
&=
\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}
\gamma^{h-1}
\mathbb E[
V^*(s_h)-r_h-\gamma V^*(s_{h+1})
].
\end{aligned}
$$

Write out the first few terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&V^*(s_1)-r_1-\gamma V^*(s_2)
\\
&\quad
+\gamma V^*(s_2)
-\gamma r_2
-\gamma^2V^*(s_3)
\\
&\quad
+\gamma^2V^*(s_3)
-\gamma^2r_3-\cdots.
\end{aligned}
$$

The $V^*(s_2),V^*(s_3),\ldots$ terms cancel.

We are left with

$$
\mathbb E[V^*(s_1)]
-
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{h=1}^\infty
\gamma^{h-1}r_h
\right].
$$

But

$$
\mathbb E[V^*(s_1)]
=
J(\pi^*)
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{h=1}^\infty
\gamma^{h-1}r_h
\right]
=
J(\widehat\pi).
$$

Therefore

$$
J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)
=
\sum_{h\ge1}\gamma^{h-1}
\mathbb E_{d_h^{\widehat\pi}}
[
V^*-Q^*(\cdot,\widehat\pi)
].
$$

This is essentially a version of the **performance-difference
telescoping argument**.

---

## 2. Replace $V^*$ by $Q^*(s,\pi^*)$

Because $\pi^*$ is optimal,

$$
V^*(s)=Q^*(s,\pi^*(s)).
$$

Therefore the quantity inside the expectation is

$$
Q^*(s,\pi^*)
-
Q^*(s,\widehat\pi).
$$

The proof then writes

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)
\le
\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{h-1}
\mathbb E_{s\sim d_h^{\widehat\pi}}
[
&Q^*(s,\pi^*)-f_k(s,\pi^*)
\\
&+f_k(s,\widehat\pi)
-Q^*(s,\widehat\pi)
].
\end{aligned}
\tag{B}
$$

Why is this an inequality rather than equality?


Start from

$$
Q^*(s,\pi^*)-Q^*(s,\widehat\pi).
$$

Insert $f_k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&Q^*(s,\pi^*)-Q^*(s,\widehat\pi)
\\
&=
Q^*(s,\pi^*)-f_k(s,\pi^*)
\\
&\quad
+
\underbrace{
f_k(s,\pi^*)-f_k(s,\widehat\pi)
}_{\le0}
\\
&\quad
+
f_k(s,\widehat\pi)-Q^*(s,\widehat\pi).
\end{aligned}
$$

Why is the middle term nonpositive?

Because $\widehat\pi$ is greedy with respect to $f_k$:

$$
f_k(s,\widehat\pi(s))
=
\max_a f_k(s,a).
$$

Therefore

$$
f_k(s,\widehat\pi)
\ge
f_k(s,\pi^*),
$$

or

$$
f_k(s,\pi^*)-f_k(s,\widehat\pi)
\le0.
$$

Dropping this negative term produces an upper bound.

This is the first important use of the fact that

$$
\widehat\pi=\pi_{f_k}.
$$

---

## 3. Convert the expectation into $L_1$ norms

The next line is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\le
\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}
\gamma^{h-1}
\bigg(
&
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{1,d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\pi^*}
\\
&+
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{1,d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\widehat\pi}
\bigg).
\end{aligned}
\tag{C}
$$



Consider the first term:

$$
\mathbb E_{s\sim d_h^{\widehat\pi}}
[
Q^*(s,\pi^*(s))-f_k(s,\pi^*(s))
].
$$

It is upper bounded by

$$
\mathbb E_{s\sim d_h^{\widehat\pi}}
[
|Q^*(s,\pi^*)-f_k(s,\pi^*)|
].
$$

But this is precisely

$$
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{1,d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\pi^*}.
$$

Here

$$
d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\pi^*
$$

means:

$$
s\sim d_h^{\widehat\pi},
\qquad
a=\pi^*(s).
$$

Similarly, the second expectation produces

$$
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{1,d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\widehat\pi}.
$$

---

## 4. Replace $L_1$ by $L_2$

The note then obtains Equation (1):

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)
\le
\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}
\gamma^{h-1}
\bigg(
&
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\pi^*}
\\
&+
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\widehat\pi}
\bigg).
\end{aligned}}
\tag{1}
$$



Recall that without a subscript $p$,

$$
\|\cdot\|_\nu
=
\|\cdot\|_{2,\nu}.
$$

Why can we say

$$
\|g\|_{1,\nu}
\le
\|g\|_{2,\nu}?
$$

Because

$$
\mathbb E_\nu[|g|]
\le
\sqrt{\mathbb E_\nu[g^2]}
$$

by Cauchy–Schwarz or Jensen.

So Equation (1) tells us:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Policy suboptimality is controlled by }
\|Q^*-f_k\|
\text{ under certain policy-induced distributions.}
}
$$

This completes part A of the proof.

Now the entire problem is reduced to understanding

$$
\|Q^*-f_k\|_{\nu\times\pi}.
$$

---

## 5. What distributions $\nu\times\pi$ matter?

The note says we now need to bound

$$
\|Q^*-f_k\|_{\nu\times\pi}
$$

for a reachable state distribution $\nu$ and an arbitrary deterministic
policy $\pi$.

There appears to be a minor numbering typo in the note here: it refers
to a distribution satisfying an earlier bullet, but the property actually
needed is the **coverage assumption from item 7**:

$$
\frac{\nu(s)}{\mu(s)}
\le C.
$$

Why do we need such a general result?

Because Equation (1) contains both

$$
d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\pi^*
$$

and

$$
d_h^{\widehat\pi}\times\widehat\pi.
$$

So rather than analyze these two special cases separately, the proof
establishes a generic bound for

$$
\nu\times\pi.
$$

---

## 6. Helper Lemma: value error from Q-function error

The note defines a special policy

$$
\boxed{
\pi_{f,f_k}(s)
=
\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A}
\max\{f(s,a),f_k(s,a)\}.
}
$$

Then Lemma 1 states

$$
\boxed{
\|V_f-V_{f_k}\|_\nu
\le
\|f-f_k\|_{\nu\times\pi_{f,f_k}}.
}
\tag{Lemma 1}
$$



This policy looks strange at first, so it is worth understanding why it
is defined this way.

Suppose for a particular state $s$,

$$
V_f(s)>V_{f_k}(s).
$$

Then the largest value among

$$
\{f(s,a),f_k(s,a):a\in\mathcal A\}
$$

must come from $f$, at an action maximizing $f$.

Thus

$$
\pi_{f,f_k}(s)
$$

selects an action maximizing $f$.

Similarly, if

$$
V_{f_k}(s)>V_f(s),
$$

the selected action maximizes $f_k$.

So this specially constructed policy selects an action where the
difference between the two maxima can be controlled by the difference
between the underlying Q-functions.

This is a **proof device**. It is not a policy FQI actually computes.

---

## 7. Proof of Lemma 1

The note starts from

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|V_f-V_{f_k}\|_\nu^2
&=
\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}
\nu(s)
\left(
\max_a f(s,a)
-
\max_{a'}f_k(s,a')
\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{D}
$$

This is simply the definition of the $L_2(\nu)$ norm.


Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\left(
\max_a f(s,a)
-
\max_{a'} f_k(s,a')
\right)^2
\\
&\le
\left(
f(s,\pi_{f,f_k}(s))
-
f_k(s,\pi_{f,f_k}(s))
\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{E}
$$

Why?

There are two cases.

If

$$
V_f(s)\ge V_{f_k}(s),
$$

then $\pi_{f,f_k}(s)$ can be chosen as an action maximizing $f$. Hence

$$
f(s,\pi_{f,f_k})=V_f(s).
$$

Meanwhile,

$$
f_k(s,\pi_{f,f_k})
\le V_{f_k}(s).
$$

Therefore

$$
f(s,\pi_{f,f_k})
-
f_k(s,\pi_{f,f_k})
\ge
V_f(s)-V_{f_k}(s).
$$

The other case is symmetric.

Thus

$$
|V_f(s)-V_{f_k}(s)|
\le
|
f(s,\pi_{f,f_k})
-
f_k(s,\pi_{f,f_k})
|.
$$

Squaring and averaging gives

$$
\|V_f-V_{f_k}\|_\nu^2
\le
\|f-f_k\|_{\nu\times\pi_{f,f_k}}^2.
$$

Taking square roots proves the lemma.

---

## 8. Begin the recursive Q-error bound

Now comes the central part of the proof.

We want

$$
\|f_k-Q^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}.
$$

The note writes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f_k-Q^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}
&=
\|
f_k-Tf_{k-1}
+
Tf_{k-1}-Q^*
\|_{\nu\times\pi}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{F}
$$



This is just adding and subtracting

$$
Tf_{k-1}.
$$

Conceptually, this decomposes the error into

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
f_k-Q^*
&=
\underbrace{f_k-Tf_{k-1}}
_{\text{FQI regression error}}
\\
&\quad+
\underbrace{Tf_{k-1}-Q^*}
_{\text{propagated previous error}}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

This decomposition is the heart of fitted-value-iteration analysis.

---

## 9. Triangle inequality

Next,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f_k-Q^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
&
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\\
&+
\|Tf_{k-1}-TQ^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{G}
$$

Why does $TQ^*$ appear?

Because

$$
Q^*=TQ^*.
$$

So

$$
Tf_{k-1}-Q^*
=
Tf_{k-1}-TQ^*.
$$

The inequality itself is simply

$$
\|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\|.
$$

---

## 10. Move the first error to the offline-data distribution

The next line is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f_k-Q^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
&
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}
\,
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
\\
&+
\gamma
\|V_{f_{k-1}}-V^*\|_{P(\nu\times\pi)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{H}
$$

This line uses **two completely different ideas**.


First,

$$
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}
\,
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}.
$$

This is exactly the coverage inequality we discussed previously.

The data distribution is

$$
\mu\times U(\mathcal A),
$$

while the policy-induced distribution is

$$
\nu\times\pi.
$$

Coverage gives

$$
\nu(s)\le C\mu(s),
$$

and uniform actions contribute the additional factor

$$
|\mathcal A|.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{
\|\cdot\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}
\|\cdot\|_{\mu\times U}.
}
$$

This is where the offline-data coverage assumption first enters the
actual proof.

---

## 11. What is $P(\nu\times\pi)$?

The note defines

$$
P(\nu\times\pi)
$$

to be the distribution of the **next state** generated by

$$
s\sim\nu,
$$

$$
a=\pi(s),
$$

$$
s'\sim P(\cdot|s,a).
$$

So

$$
\boxed{
s'\sim P(\nu\times\pi)
}
$$

is shorthand for this three-stage process.

Formally,

$$
P(\nu\times\pi)(s')
=
\sum_{s,a}
\nu(s)\pi(a|s)P(s'|s,a).
$$

For deterministic $\pi$,

$$
P(\nu\times\pi)(s')
=
\sum_s
\nu(s)
P(s'|s,\pi(s)).
$$

If $\nu$ is a reachable state distribution, then

$$
P(\nu\times\pi)
$$

is also reachable: it is simply the state distribution one time step
later after taking action according to $\pi$.

This fact is crucial because it means the coverage assumption can be
used again at the next recursive step.

---

## 12. The starred step

The note separately proves

$$
\boxed{
\|Tf_{k-1}-TQ^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
\gamma
\|V_{f_{k-1}}-V^*\|_{P(\nu\times\pi)}.
}
\tag{*}
$$

Let's derive every line.

Start by squaring:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\|Tf_{k-1}-TQ^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}^2
\\
&=
\mathbb E_{(s,a)\sim\nu\times\pi}
\left[
\left(
(Tf_{k-1})(s,a)
-
(TQ^*)(s,a)
\right)^2
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Now expand the Bellman operators.

We have

$$
(Tf_{k-1})(s,a)
=
\mathbb E[
r+\gamma V_{f_{k-1}}(s')
|s,a
]
$$

and

$$
(TQ^*)(s,a)
=
\mathbb E[
r+\gamma V^*(s')
|s,a
].
$$

The reward terms cancel:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(Tf_{k-1})(s,a)
-
(TQ^*)(s,a)
=
\gamma
\mathbb E_{s'\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}
[
V_{f_{k-1}}(s')-V^*(s')
].
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\|Tf_{k-1}-TQ^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}^2
\\
&=
\mathbb E_{(s,a)\sim\nu\times\pi}
\left[
\left(
\gamma
\mathbb E_{s'|s,a}
[
V_{f_{k-1}}(s')-V^*(s')
]
\right)^2
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

---

## 13. Jensen's inequality

The next step is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\le
\gamma^2
\mathbb E_{
(s,a)\sim\nu\times\pi,\,
s'\sim P(\cdot|s,a)
}
\left[
\left(
V_{f_{k-1}}(s')-V^*(s')
\right)^2
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is Jensen's inequality:

$$
(\mathbb E[X])^2
\le
\mathbb E[X^2].
$$

Specifically, let

$$
X=
V_{f_{k-1}}(s')-V^*(s').
$$

Then

$$
\left(
\mathbb E[X|s,a]
\right)^2
\le
\mathbb E[X^2|s,a].
$$

That is exactly what turns an expectation **inside a square** into an
expectation **of a square**.

---

## 14. Recognize the next-state distribution

Now

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\gamma^2
\mathbb E_{
(s,a)\sim\nu\times\pi,\,
s'\sim P(\cdot|s,a)
}
[
(V_{f_{k-1}}(s')-V^*(s'))^2
]
\\
&=
\gamma^2
\mathbb E_{
s'\sim P(\nu\times\pi)
}
[
(V_{f_{k-1}}(s')-V^*(s'))^2
].
\end{aligned}
$$

By definition, this is

$$
\gamma^2
\|V_{f_{k-1}}-V^*\|_
{P(\nu\times\pi)}^2.
$$

Taking square roots gives

$$
\|Tf_{k-1}-TQ^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
\gamma
\|V_{f_{k-1}}-V^*\|_
{P(\nu\times\pi)}.
$$

This proves step $(*)$.

---

## 15. Use Lemma 1 to turn value error back into Q-error

The next line of the main proof is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f_k-Q^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
&
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}
\,
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
\\
&+
\gamma
\|f_{k-1}-Q^*\|_
{
P(\nu\times\pi)
\times
\pi_{f_{k-1},Q^*}
}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{I}
$$



This is Lemma 1 with

$$
f=f_{k-1}
$$

and

$$
f_k \text{ in the lemma replaced by } Q^*.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\|V_{f_{k-1}}-V^*\|_{P(\nu\times\pi)}
\le
\|f_{k-1}-Q^*\|_
{
P(\nu\times\pi)
\times\pi_{f_{k-1},Q^*}
}.
$$

This is extremely important because now the RHS has exactly the same
form as the quantity we originally wanted to bound:

$$
\|f_j-Q^*\|_{\text{reachable distribution}\times\text{policy}}.
$$

Only $j$ has changed from $k$ to $k-1$.

Therefore the inequality can be applied **recursively**.

---

## 16. The recursive structure

For intuition, define

$$
e_k(\nu,\pi)
=
\|f_k-Q^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}.
$$

Then the previous result says approximately

$$
\boxed{
e_k
\le
\underbrace{
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
}_{\text{new regression error}}
+
\gamma e_{k-1}.
}
\tag{J}
$$

This is the central recursion of the proof.

It says:

> The error at iteration $k$ consists of the error made by the current
> regression plus the previous Q-function error, discounted by
> $\gamma$.

So now everything depends on bounding

$$
\boxed{
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}.
}
$$

This is part C of the proof.

---

## 17. Why squared regression loss gives exactly Bellman error

The note writes

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}^2
&=
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}(f_k;f_{k-1})
\\
&\quad-
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}
(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1}).
\end{aligned}}
\tag{K}
$$



This identity is one of the most useful things to understand in the
whole proof.

Define the regression target

$$
Y
=
r+\gamma V_{f_{k-1}}(s').
$$

Conditioned on $(s,a)$,

$$
\mathbb E[Y|s,a]
=
(Tf_{k-1})(s,a).
$$

Let

$$
m(s,a)=Tf_{k-1}(s,a).
$$

For any candidate $f$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[(f-Y)^2]
&=
\mathbb E[
(f-m+m-Y)^2
]
\\
&=
\mathbb E[(f-m)^2]
+
\mathbb E[(m-Y)^2]
\\
&\quad+
2\mathbb E[(f-m)(m-Y)].
\end{aligned}
$$

The cross term vanishes because

$$
\mathbb E[m-Y|s,a]=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[(f-Y)^2]
=
\mathbb E[(f-m)^2]
+
\mathbb E[(m-Y)^2].
$$

Rearranging,

$$
\mathbb E[(f-m)^2]
=
\mathbb E[(f-Y)^2]
-
\mathbb E[(m-Y)^2].
$$

Set

$$
f=f_k,
\qquad
m=Tf_{k-1}.
$$

Then

$$
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}^2
=
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}(f_k;f_{k-1})
-
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}
(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1}).
$$

This is sometimes called the **Pythagorean property of squared loss**.

---

## 18. Replace population losses by empirical losses

Using the uniform deviation assumption,

$$
|
\mathcal L_D(f;f')
-
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}(f;f')
|
\le\epsilon,
$$

the note obtains

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}(f_k;f_{k-1})
-
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}
(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1})
\\
&\le
\mathcal L_D(f_k;f_{k-1})
-
\mathcal L_D(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1})
+
2\epsilon.
\end{aligned}
\tag{L}
$$



Why $2\epsilon$?

For the first term,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}(f_k;f_{k-1})
\le
\mathcal L_D(f_k;f_{k-1})+\epsilon.
$$

For the second,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mu\times U}
(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1})
\ge
\mathcal L_D(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1})-\epsilon.
$$

Subtracting gives

$$
+\epsilon+\epsilon=2\epsilon.
$$

---

## 19. Where Bellman completeness is used

The note puts beside this line

$$
Tf_{k-1}\in\mathcal F.
$$

That is exactly Assumption 3:

$$
f\in\mathcal F
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Tf\in\mathcal F.
$$

Why do we need it here?

The uniform-deviation statement was assumed only for

$$
f,f'\in\mathcal F.
$$

We already know

$$
f_k,f_{k-1}\in\mathcal F.
$$

But in order to apply it to

$$
Tf_{k-1},
$$

we also need

$$
Tf_{k-1}\in\mathcal F.
$$

So this is the precise location where **Bellman completeness** enters
the main proof.

---

## 20. Use empirical optimality of $f_k$

By the FQI definition,

$$
f_k
=
\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal F}
\mathcal L_D(f;f_{k-1}).
$$

Since

$$
Tf_{k-1}\in\mathcal F,
$$

it is a valid competitor.

Therefore

$$
\mathcal L_D(f_k;f_{k-1})
\le
\mathcal L_D(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1}).
$$

Hence

$$
\mathcal L_D(f_k;f_{k-1})
-
\mathcal L_D(Tf_{k-1};f_{k-1})
\le0.
$$

Plugging this into (L),

$$
\boxed{
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}^2
\le2\epsilon.
}
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
\le
\sqrt{2\epsilon}.
}
\tag{M}
$$



This is the one-step FQI statistical error.

---

## 21. Plug it into the recursive error bound

Recall

$$
e_k
\le
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}
\,
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
+
\gamma e_{k-1}.
$$

Using

$$
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
\le
\sqrt{2\epsilon},
$$

we obtain

$$
e_k
\le
\boxed{
\sqrt{2|\mathcal A|C\epsilon}
}
+
\gamma e_{k-1}.
$$

Let

$$
B=
\sqrt{2|\mathcal A|C\epsilon}.
$$

Then

$$
e_k\le B+\gamma e_{k-1}.
$$

Apply it again:

$$
e_k
\le
B+\gamma(B+\gamma e_{k-2})
$$

so

$$
e_k
\le
B+\gamma B+\gamma^2e_{k-2}.
$$

Again,

$$
e_k
\le
B+\gamma B+\gamma^2B+\gamma^3e_{k-3}.
$$

After $k$ expansions,

$$
e_k
\le
B
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\gamma^j
+
\gamma^k e_0.
$$

The geometric series is

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\gamma^j
=
\frac{1-\gamma^k}{1-\gamma}.
$$

Therefore

$$
e_k
\le
\frac{1-\gamma^k}{1-\gamma}
B
+
\gamma^ke_0.
$$

---

## 22. Bound the initial error $e_0$

The algorithm initializes

$$
f_0\equiv0.
$$

Assuming rewards are bounded by $R_{\max}$,

$$
|Q^*(s,a)|
\le
\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}.
$$

Hence

$$
\|f_0-Q^*\|
=
\|Q^*\|
\le
\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\|f_k-Q^*\|_{\nu\times\pi}
\le
\frac{1-\gamma^k}{1-\gamma}
\sqrt{2|\mathcal A|C\epsilon}
+
\gamma^k
\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}.
}
\tag{N}
$$

This is exactly the bound stated in the note.


There are two qualitatively different errors here:

$$
\underbrace{
\frac{1-\gamma^k}{1-\gamma}
\sqrt{2|\mathcal A|C\epsilon}
}_{\text{statistical / regression error}}
$$

and

$$
\underbrace{
\gamma^k
\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
}_{\text{finite-iteration error}}.
$$

The second disappears exponentially fast as $k\to\infty$.

The first does not disappear with more FQI iterations because it comes
from finite offline data.

---

## 23. Return to Equation (1)

Equation (1) gave

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(\pi^*)-J(\pi_{f_k})
\le
\sum_{h=1}^\infty
\gamma^{h-1}
\big(
&
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{d_h^{\pi_{f_k}}\times\pi^*}
\\
&+
\|Q^*-f_k\|_
{d_h^{\pi_{f_k}}\times\pi_{f_k}}
\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Each of the two norms satisfies bound (N).

Call that common upper bound $E_k$:

$$
E_k
=
\frac{1-\gamma^k}{1-\gamma}
\sqrt{2|\mathcal A|C\epsilon}
+
\gamma^k
\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}.
$$

There are two terms, so

$$
J(\pi^*)-J(\pi_{f_k})
\le
2E_k
\sum_{h=1}^\infty\gamma^{h-1}.
$$

And

$$
\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{h-1}
=
\frac1{1-\gamma}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
J(\pi^*)-J(\pi_{f_k})
\le
\frac{2}{1-\gamma}
\left[
\frac{1-\gamma^k}{1-\gamma}
\sqrt{2|\mathcal A|C\epsilon}
+
\gamma^k
\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
\right].
}
\tag{O}
$$

That is the main result on page 3.

---

## 24. The entire main proof in one chain

The logical flow is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
J(\pi^*)-J(\widehat\pi)
&\lesssim
\sum_h\gamma^{h-1}
\|Q^*-f_k\|_{\text{policy dist.}}
\\[1mm]
&\lesssim
\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
\|Q^*-f_k\|_{\text{policy dist.}}
\\[1mm]
\|Q^*-f_k\|_{\text{policy dist.}}
&\le
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
\\
&\quad+
\gamma
\|Q^*-f_{k-1}\|_{\text{next policy dist.}}
\\[1mm]
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|_{\mu\times U}
&\le
\sqrt{2\epsilon}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

So the key transformation is

$$
\boxed{
\text{finite-sample regression error}
\rightarrow
\text{Bellman error}
\rightarrow
\text{Q-function error}
\rightarrow
\text{policy performance error}.
}
$$

---

## 25. Why does the first analysis only give an $n^{-1/4}$ rate?

The note then points out a weakness.

Suppose the uniform loss deviation is obtained using Hoeffding:

$$
\epsilon
=
O\left(
\frac1{\sqrt n}
\right).
$$

But the Bellman error satisfies

$$
\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|
\le
\sqrt{2\epsilon}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\sqrt{\epsilon}
=
O(n^{-1/4}).
$$

So the final policy error scales only as

$$
O(n^{-1/4}).
$$

The remainder of the note improves this to

$$
O(n^{-1/2})
$$

using a **fast-rate excess-risk argument based on Bernstein's
inequality**.

---

## 26. Fast-rate proof: define excess loss

Fix some previous function $f'$.

The note defines

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y(f;f')
:=
&
\left(
f(s,a)-r-\gamma V_{f'}(s')
\right)^2
\\
&-
\left(
(Tf')(s,a)-r-\gamma V_{f'}(s')
\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{P}
$$



This is simply

$$
\boxed{
Y
=
\text{loss of }f
-
\text{loss of the ideal Bellman regressor }Tf'.
}
$$

So $Y$ measures **excess squared loss**.

For every data sample, we get

$$
Y_i(f;f').
$$

Then

$$
\frac1n
\sum_{i=1}^n
Y_i(f;f')
=
\mathcal L_D(f;f')
-
\mathcal L_D(Tf';f').
$$

The second term does not depend on $f$, so minimizing

$$
\mathcal L_D(f;f')
$$

is equivalent to minimizing

$$
\frac1n\sum_iY_i(f;f').
$$

---

## 27. Expected excess loss equals squared Bellman error

The note obtains

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E[Y(f;f')]
=
\|f-Tf'\|_{\mu\times U}^2.
}
\tag{Q}
$$



This is exactly the same squared-loss identity we derived earlier:

$$
\mathcal L(f)-\mathcal L(Tf')
=
\|f-Tf'\|^2.
$$

Therefore, if we can prove

$$
\mathbb E[Y(\widehat T_{\mathcal F}f';f')]
=
O(1/n),
$$

then

$$
\|\widehat T_{\mathcal F}f'-Tf'\|^2
=
O(1/n),
$$

and consequently

$$
\boxed{
\|\widehat T_{\mathcal F}f'-Tf'\|
=
O(n^{-1/2}).
}
$$

That is the desired fast rate.

---

## 28. Why Bernstein can give a faster rate

The crucial observation is that the variance of $Y$ becomes smaller
when the expected excess loss becomes smaller.

The note derives

$$
\operatorname{Var}[Y(f;f')]
\le
4V_{\max}^2
\mathbb E[Y(f;f')].
\tag{R}
$$



Let's derive this carefully.

First,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y)
\le
\mathbb E[Y^2].
$$

Let

$$
z=r+\gamma V_{f'}(s')
$$

and

$$
m=(Tf')(s,a).
$$

Then

$$
Y=(f-z)^2-(m-z)^2.
$$

Use

$$
x^2-y^2=(x-y)(x+y).
$$

Therefore

$$
Y
=
(f-m)(f+m-2z).
$$

Hence

$$
Y^2
=
(f-m)^2(f+m-2z)^2.
$$

Under the boundedness assumptions used by the note,

$$
|f+m-2z|
\le2V_{\max},
$$

so

$$
Y^2
\le
4V_{\max}^2(f-m)^2.
$$

Taking expectations,

$$
\mathbb E[Y^2]
\le
4V_{\max}^2
\mathbb E[(f-Tf')^2].
$$

But from (Q),

$$
\mathbb E[(f-Tf')^2]
=
\mathbb E[Y].
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(Y)
\le
4V_{\max}^2\mathbb E[Y].
}
$$

This is called a **Bernstein condition** or
**self-bounding variance property**.

It is what permits the $1/n$ excess-risk rate.

---

## 29. Apply Bernstein's inequality

Let

$$
N=|\mathcal F|.
$$

For fixed $f'$, Bernstein plus a union bound gives, simultaneously for
all $f\in\mathcal F$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[Y(f;f')]
-
\frac1n\sum_iY_i(f;f')
\le
&
\sqrt{
\frac{
8V_{\max}^2
\mathbb E[Y(f;f')]
\log(N/\delta)
}{n}
}
\\
&+
\frac{
4V_{\max}^2\log(N/\delta)
}{3n}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{S}
$$



Notice the crucial difference from Hoeffding.

Hoeffding would give roughly

$$
O\left(
\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{n}}
\right)
$$

regardless of how small the true error is.

Bernstein gives

$$
O\left(
\sqrt{
\frac{
\mathbb E[Y]\log N
}{n}
}
+
\frac{\log N}{n}
\right).
$$

So the deviation becomes smaller as

$$
\mathbb E[Y]
$$

itself becomes smaller.

That is what creates the fast rate.

---

## 30. Use the fact that the empirical minimizer beats $Tf'$

Let

$$
\widehat f
=
\widehat T_{\mathcal F}f'.
$$

Since $\widehat f$ minimizes empirical squared loss,

$$
\frac1n
\sum_iY_i(\widehat f;f')
\le
\frac1n
\sum_iY_i(Tf';f').
$$

But

$$
Y_i(Tf';f')=0
$$

for every sample, because both terms in its definition become identical.

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\frac1n
\sum_iY_i(\widehat f;f')
\le0.
}
\tag{T}
$$



This is a particularly strong property of realizable squared regression.

---

## 31. Apply Bernstein to the empirical minimizer

Because inequality (S) holds simultaneously for all $f$, it also holds
for the data-dependent choice

$$
f=\widehat f.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[Y(\widehat f;f')]
\le
&
\sqrt{
\frac{
8V_{\max}^2
\mathbb E[Y(\widehat f;f')]
\log(N/\delta)
}{n}
}
\\
&+
\frac{
4V_{\max}^2\log(N/\delta)
}{3n}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{U}
$$

The empirical term disappeared because of (T).

Now let

$$
x=
\mathbb E[Y(\widehat f;f')]
$$

and

$$
c=
\frac{V_{\max}^2\log(N/\delta)}{n}.
$$

Then

$$
x\le\sqrt{8cx}+\frac43c.
$$

Set

$$
t=\sqrt{x/c}.
$$

Then

$$
t^2
\le
\sqrt8\,t+\frac43.
$$

Solving the quadratic yields

$$
t
\le
\sqrt2+\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
x
\le
\left(
\sqrt2+\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}
\right)^2
\frac{
V_{\max}^2\log(N/\delta)
}{n}.
}
\tag{V}
$$

This is exactly the final inequality in the note.


Since

$$
x
=
\|\widehat T_{\mathcal F}f'-Tf'\|_{\mu\times U}^2,
$$

we get

$$
\boxed{
\|\widehat T_{\mathcal F}f'-Tf'\|_{\mu\times U}
=
O\left(
V_{\max}
\sqrt{
\frac{\log(|\mathcal F|/\delta)}{n}
}
\right).
}
$$

So the Bellman regression error is now $O(n^{-1/2})$, rather than
$O(n^{-1/4})$.

---

## 32. One technical subtlety worth noticing

There is a small rigor issue worth keeping in mind if you want to reuse
this proof in your own work.

The fast-rate section states the Bernstein result **for fixed $f'$**.
But in FQI we eventually substitute

$$
f'=f_{k-1},
$$

and $f_{k-1}$ is itself computed from the same dataset.

So $f_{k-1}$ is data-dependent.

A clean way to make this fully uniform is to union-bound over

$$
(f,f')\in\mathcal F\times\mathcal F
$$

instead of only over $f$. Then the logarithmic factor becomes roughly

$$
\log\frac{|\mathcal F|^2}{\delta}
=
2\log|\mathcal F|+\log\frac1\delta,
$$

which does **not** change the $n^{-1/2}$ rate.

This is especially relevant if you want to adapt this proof to another
FQI estimator.

## 33. Where every major assumption was used

It may help to map the assumptions directly onto the proof:

| Assumption                | Where it enters                                             |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Finite $\mathcal F$     | Union bounds for uniform concentration                      |
| $Q^*\in\mathcal F$      | No approximation error at the optimal solution              |
| $Tf\in\mathcal F$       | Allows $Tf_{k-1}$ to be the regression comparator         |
| Data from $\mu\times U$ | Determines the regression norm                              |
| Coverage $C$            | Converts $\mu\times U$ error to policy-distribution error |
| Greedy $\pi_{f_k}$      | Lets us drop $f_k(\pi^*)-f_k(\widehat\pi)\le0$            |
| Uniform deviation         | Gives $\|f_k-Tf_{k-1}\|^2\le2\epsilon$                    |
| Squared loss              | Gives the exact excess-risk/Bellman-error identity          |
| Bounded values            | Needed for Hoeffding/Bernstein and $V_{\max}$             |

## 34. The proof's deepest idea

The central quantity is not directly

$$
\|f_k-Q^*\|.
$$

Instead, the proof first asks:

$$
\boxed{
\text{How accurately does each FQI step approximate its Bellman target?}
}
$$

That quantity is

$$
\delta_k
=
f_k-Tf_{k-1}.
$$

The recursion is essentially

$$
\boxed{
\|f_k-Q^*\|
\lesssim
\underbrace{
\sqrt{|\mathcal A|C}\|\delta_k\|
}_{\text{current fitting error}}
+
\underbrace{
\gamma\|f_{k-1}-Q^*\|
}_{\text{previous error}}.
}
$$

Repeatedly expanding gives

$$
\boxed{
\text{final Q-error}
\approx
\delta_k
+
\gamma\delta_{k-1}
+
\gamma^2\delta_{k-2}
+\cdots.
}
$$

Then the policy-performance argument adds another geometric factor

$$
\frac1{1-\gamma}.
$$

That is why FQI theory is often described as an analysis of
**Bellman-error propagation**.

This structure is also the part you would want to preserve if you
replace the ordinary max Bellman target with something such as a
log-average-exp or soft-Q backup: the main questions become whether the
new operator has an appropriate fixed point/contraction property, how
Lemma 1 changes, and whether the regression-error identity still lets
you control the one-step operator error.

## Current State

The slow-rate ($n^{-1/4}$) and fast-rate (Bernstein) arguments worked through here are the standard finite-sample account of FQI under Bellman completeness and uniform concentrability, and they remain the baseline later refinements are stated against. What has moved on is the coverage assumption: uniform $C$ has largely given way to single-policy concentrability, and the pessimistic descendant [[fitted-q-iteration-pessimistic]] carries instance-dependent bounds this analysis cannot express. Read as foundations, not as the frontier.
